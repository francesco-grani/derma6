# Ceramides Profile

## Overview

Ceramides are a family of lipid molecules (sphingolipids) that form the dominant structural component of the stratum corneum — the outermost layer of the skin. Alongside cholesterol and free fatty acids, ceramides make up the intercellular lipid matrix of the stratum corneum, composing approximately **50% of its lipid content** (with cholesterol at ~25% and fatty acids at ~15%). Together these three components form a "lamellar bilayer" structure often compared to mortar between bricks, where the corneocytes (dead skin cells) are the bricks.

This lipid matrix serves two critical functions:
1. Preventing trans-epidermal water loss (TEWL) — keeping moisture inside the skin.
2. Acting as a physical and chemical barrier against pathogens, allergens, and irritants.

## Types of Ceramides

Ceramides are identified by their headgroup chemistry and acyl chain composition. INCI nomenclature uses letters and numbers:

| INCI Name | Traditional Name | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Ceramide NP | Ceramide 3 | Most abundant in healthy skin; critical for barrier integrity |
| Ceramide AP | Ceramide 6-II | Supports cohesion between corneocytes |
| Ceramide EOP | Ceramide 9 | Ester-linked omega-hydroxyl fatty acid; important for barrier function |
| Ceramide NS | Ceramide 2 | Supports water-holding capacity |
| Ceramide AS | Ceramide 1 | Involved in lamellar body formation |

Well-formulated topical products typically include a **blend of ceramide types**, since the skin barrier requires multiple species in proportion to function optimally. The most common and well-studied combination includes Ceramide NP, AP, and EOP.

## Mechanism of Action

When the skin barrier is disrupted (by aggressive cleansing, over-exfoliation, environmental exposure, eczema, or natural ageing), ceramide levels in the stratum corneum decline. Topical ceramide delivery replenishes these depleted lipids in two ways:

1. **Direct barrier incorporation:** exogenous ceramides integrate into existing lamellar structures between corneocytes, physically reinforcing the barrier.
2. **Stimulation of endogenous synthesis:** the presence of exogenous lipids upregulates the skin's own ceramide synthesis pathways, creating a self-reinforcing effect with continued use.

The optimal vehicle for ceramide delivery is a **lipid-rich cream or lotion**, often formulated with cholesterol and fatty acids in a physiologically relevant ratio to match the skin's natural lipid composition.

## Benefits

- Restores and maintains a compromised skin barrier.
- Reduces TEWL, improving skin hydration and softness.
- Reduces sensitivity, redness, and reactivity in conditions like eczema (atopic dermatitis), psoriasis, and rosacea.
- Protects against environmental irritants and pathogens.
- Fundamental for dry, sensitive, and dehydrated skin types.
- Counteracts the barrier disruption caused by retinoids and acids.

## How to Use

- Apply as a moisturiser or as part of a moisturiser after serums and treatments.
- Use AM and/or PM — especially PM to support overnight barrier repair.
- On nights when retinol or acids are used, a ceramide-rich moisturiser is strongly recommended to counteract the increased TEWL.
- No wait time required — ceramides are compatible with everything.

## Compatibility

Ceramides are compatible with every common skincare ingredient:
- Safe with retinol, AHAs, BHAs, benzoyl peroxide, vitamin C, niacinamide, hyaluronic acid, peptides, SPF.
- Particularly beneficial after acid exfoliation or retinoid use to restore barrier function.
- Suitable for sensitive skin, eczema-prone skin, and post-procedure skin.

## Key Products and Formulations

Look for formulations that list ceramides alongside their cofactors:
- **Cholesterol + ceramides + fatty acids** in a 1:1:1–3 molar ratio is optimal for barrier repair.
- Ceramide-containing brands frequently cited in dermatology: CeraVe, Cetaphil, La Roche-Posay.
