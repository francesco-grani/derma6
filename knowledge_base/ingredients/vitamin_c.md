# Vitamin C Profile

## Overview

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is a potent antioxidant and one of the most studied topical actives for brightening, photoprotection, and anti-ageing. It exists in multiple forms with different stability and efficacy profiles. Understanding the differences between forms is critical for selecting an effective product.

## Forms of Vitamin C

### L-Ascorbic Acid (L-AA)
The most bioavailable and most studied form. Concentrations of 10–20% are clinically effective. It is the only form that works directly on the skin without conversion. Highly unstable — oxidises rapidly when exposed to light, air, or alkaline pH, turning the product orange/brown and rendering it inactive. Requires a formulation pH of 3.5 or below for optimal skin penetration via the lipid bilayer.

### Vitamin C Derivatives
More stable but require enzymatic conversion in the skin before becoming active, making them less potent:
- **Ascorbyl glucoside (AA2G):** water-soluble, stable, converted to L-AA by glucosidase in skin.
- **Sodium ascorbyl phosphate (SAP):** water-soluble, stable, popular in acne-control formulations (mild antimicrobial properties).
- **Ascorbyl tetraisopalmitate (ATIP):** oil-soluble, penetrates well, stable; good for dry skin formulations.
- **3-O-Ethyl ascorbic acid:** relatively new; combines stability with reasonably good bioavailability.

## Mechanism of Action

1. **Antioxidant activity:** donates electrons to neutralise reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated by UV exposure, pollution, and metabolic processes. Also regenerates vitamin E (tocopherol), amplifying UV protection when the two are combined.
2. **Collagen synthesis:** serves as an essential cofactor for prolyl and lysyl hydroxylases — enzymes required to stabilise the collagen triple helix. Without vitamin C, collagen is structurally defective.
3. **Melanin inhibition:** inhibits tyrosinase, the rate-limiting enzyme in melanin biosynthesis, reducing hyperpigmentation and post-inflammatory dark spots.
4. **Photoprotection (indirect):** neutralises ROS that would otherwise cause UV-induced DNA damage. Does not replace SPF.

## How to Use

- **Time of day:** AM is preferred — antioxidant defence is most useful before UV and environmental exposure.
- **Apply before SPF** in the AM routine, after cleansing and water-based serums.
- **Storage:** keep L-AA formulations in opaque, airtight containers away from heat and light. Discard if product has turned orange-brown.
- **AM + SPF synergy:** vitamin C + vitamin E + ferulic acid (a well-studied combination) roughly doubles the photoprotective effect of SPF.

## Concentrations

- L-ascorbic acid: 10% is the minimum clinically effective dose; 15–20% is common; diminishing returns above 20%.
- Derivatives: effective at lower percentages due to different absorption dynamics.

## Conflicts and Cautions

- **With retinol:** L-AA (low pH) can degrade retinol if layered directly. Separate them — vitamin C in AM, retinol in PM.
- **With niacinamide:** generally safe at typical formulation concentrations (see Niacinamide Profile).
- **With benzoyl peroxide:** avoid combining — oxidation reduces efficacy of both.
- **With copper peptides:** vitamin C can oxidise copper ions, reducing peptide efficacy. Use separately.
- **Sensitivity:** at pH <3.5 (required for L-AA), some users experience stinging, especially on compromised or sensitive skin.
